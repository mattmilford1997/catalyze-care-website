What Does It Cost to Acquire a Patient?

By Andrew · Updated December 7, 2026·3 min read

Every practice owner wants to know one number: what does a new patient cost? The honest answer is a range, driven by specialty, channel, market, and how good the intake process is. Here are the ranges, with the caveats.

The definitions first

Cost per lead: marketing spend divided by inquiries (calls, forms, messages).

Cost per booked patient: spend divided by patients who scheduled a first visit.

Cost per active patient: spend divided by patients who completed a first visit and are in care.

The gap between these is where most practices lose money. A $60 lead that books 30 percent of the time is a $200 booked patient. If half of those no-show, it's a $400 active patient. Improving intake conversion is often cheaper than improving media.

Paid search (Google)

Ranges we see for cost per booked patient on paid ads for healthcare practices:

  • Outpatient therapy (non-prescribing): $100 to $350. Lower in markets with less competition, higher in major metros.
  • Psychiatry and medication management: $200 to $600. Certification requirements and competition raise CPCs.
  • ABA: $300 to $900. Long intake cycles, insurance authorization, and parent decision timelines push cost up, but lifetime value is high.
  • Pediatric speech, OT, PT: $150 to $500. Depends heavily on Medicaid acceptance and local supply.
  • Specialty telehealth (concierge, longevity, weight management): $150 to $800, with wide variance by offer and price point.

Paid social (Meta)

Typically 30 to 60 percent lower cost per lead than Google, with lower intent and lower booking rates, so cost per booked patient often lands in a similar range. Meta works best for services with an awareness gap and for parent-decided care.

Provider referrals

Harder to benchmark because cost is front-loaded and value compounds. That is the economics behind healthcare referral marketing. A reasonable model: a market campaign that costs a few thousand dollars over 90 days and produces 10 to 25 referring offices; by month six, cost per referred patient is commonly below paid CAC and falling as offices keep referring without additional spend. Referred patients also convert to active at higher rates (often 60 to 80 percent booked-to-active vs. 40 to 60 percent for paid) because they arrive with a clinical reason and a warm introduction.

SEO and directories

Near-zero marginal cost per patient once established, with a long ramp. Psychology Today alone can produce booked patients at $30 to $100 for many therapy practices. Organic search for a practice that ranks for state and city terms produces patients at a blended cost that falls every month.

What moves the numbers

  • Insurance acceptance. Medicaid-accepting practices have higher lead volume and lower cost per lead in most markets, and higher no-show risk without a strong intake process.
  • Speed to first visit. Every day between inquiry and appointment lowers conversion. Practices that see patients within a week convert dramatically better.
  • Intake responsiveness. Calls answered live, forms followed up within an hour, evening availability. This is the cheapest CAC lever there is.
  • Market competition. Behavioral health CPCs in Los Angeles and Chicago are multiples of those in secondary markets.
  • Landing page and offer clarity. Insurance accepted, wait time, and how to start, above the fold.

How to know if yours are good

Compare cost per active patient to first-year revenue per patient. In outpatient behavioral health, a patient in weekly therapy for six months is several thousand dollars in revenue. A $300 acquisition cost on that is excellent. For ABA, first-year revenue per patient can be tens of thousands, which justifies a much higher CAC. Set your target CAC from lifetime value, not from what the ad platform says a lead should cost.
