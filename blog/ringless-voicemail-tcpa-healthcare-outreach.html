Voicemail Drops, TCPA, and Healthcare Outreach

By Andrew · Updated September 14, 2026·3 min read

Voicemail to the office manager is often the single highest-response touch in a referral campaign. It's also the channel with the most legal nuance. Here's how to think about it.

What the TCPA actually restricts

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act restricts calls made with an automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice to cell phones, and prerecorded telemarketing calls to residential lines, without the required consent. In 2022, the FCC issued a declaratory ruling confirming that ringless voicemail delivered to a wireless number is a "call" using a prerecorded voice, which put it squarely inside the TCPA. Courts have treated AI-generated voice the same as prerecorded.

Penalties are per call: statutory damages of $500 per violation, up to $1,500 if willful, and TCPA class actions are a cottage industry.

Why B2B healthcare outreach is different, and why that's not a free pass

Most referral outreach targets a physician office's main business line, which is a landline in a business location, and the message is about clinical referral availability, not a consumer product. That combination reduces TCPA exposure considerably compared to consumer marketing to cell phones. But:

  • Some office numbers are cell phones. Solo practices and mobile providers often publish a mobile number. Calling a cell with a prerecorded voice without consent is a violation regardless of the business context.
  • State laws add restrictions. Several states have their own telemarketing rules, and some (New Jersey has proposed this) specifically target ringless voicemail.
  • Do Not Call. Business numbers generally aren't on the National DNC Registry, but state registries and internal opt-outs still apply.
  • Caller ID and identification. Every call must display a valid callable number and identify the caller.

The practical rule: know what kind of number you're calling before you decide how to call it.

How we run voicemail compliantly

  1. Line-type scrubbing. Before every campaign, every number is checked against a carrier database to classify it as landline, VoIP, or wireless. Wireless numbers are excluded from prerecorded voicemail drops unless we have documented consent.
  2. Business lines only. Campaigns target published office main lines, not personal numbers.
  3. Content is informational and identified. The message states who's calling, what the practice does, who it accepts, and a callback number. No pressure, no consumer-style pitch.
  4. Opt-out honored across channels. An office that asks not to be contacted is suppressed for voicemail, fax, and email together, for every client. The FCC's direction is that a revocation on one channel applies to all, and we run it that way regardless of the current effective date.
  5. Time-of-day windows. Business hours in the recipient's time zone.
  6. Records. Every drop is logged with number, line type, content, and timestamp.

What a good voicemail says

Twenty to thirty seconds. Addressed to the office manager or referral coordinator by role. Who we are, what patients we take, one concrete reason it's useful to them (short wait times, accepts a specific Medicaid plan, a service they can't find), and a direct callback number. Followed by a fax that lands the same week so the coordinator has something in hand when they call back.

Response rates for well-targeted office voicemail commonly run several times higher than cold email, and callbacks are usually from exactly the person who controls referrals.

The question to ask any vendor

"How do you classify line types before you drop, and what do you do with wireless numbers?" If the answer is vague, the vendor is transferring TCPA risk to you.
