The Pediatrician Channel

By Andrew · Updated November 23, 2026·3 min read

Pediatricians are the highest-value referral source for pediatric behavioral health, ABA, speech, and OT practices, and most of them are underserved. Their offices field constant parent questions about speech, behavior, attention, and anxiety, and their referral options are often a months-long waitlist or a name from memory. A practice that makes referring easy becomes the default. Here's how.

What pediatricians actually need

Talk to a pediatric office manager and you'll hear the same things:

  • Someone who will actually see the patient soon. Waitlists of three to six months are the norm in pediatric therapy. A practice that can see a child in one to two weeks is remarkable.
  • Someone who takes the child's insurance. A huge share of pediatric patients are on Medicaid or CHIP. If you're in network with the state's Medicaid managed care plans, lead with it.
  • A simple referral path. A one-page fax-back form or a link. Not a portal login.
  • Feedback. Did the child get seen? What did you find? Pediatricians are managing the whole child and want to know.
  • No hassle for the family. The office doesn't want to field calls from parents who couldn't get through to you.

Every message to a pediatric office should hit at least three of these.

Timing matters

Pediatric referrals cluster around well visits and school transitions. Developmental screening at 18, 24, and 30 months surfaces speech and social concerns. Back-to-school visits (August and September) surface attention and behavior. Kindergarten and middle school transitions generate anxiety and adjustment referrals. Time outreach waves to land just before those windows.

The sequence that works with pediatric offices

Touch 1: Fax. A referral form styled like the ones they already use. Headline: what you treat, ages, insurance accepted, current wait time. Fax-back form below. Sent to the main office fax.

Touch 2: Voicemail to the office manager three to five days later. Thirty seconds: who you are, that you sent a referral form, that you're seeing kids within two weeks, direct callback number.

Touch 3: Materials for the office. For offices that respond, a small supply of printed referral forms and parent handouts (what to expect, how to schedule) that the medical assistant can hand to a family at the visit. This is what turns a one-time referral into a habit.

Touch 4: A clinician-to-clinician call or visit for the top offices. Ten minutes between your clinical lead and the pediatrician builds a relationship that outlasts any campaign.

Ongoing: closed-loop notes after every referred child's first visit, and a quarterly update with capacity, new services, and clinician additions.

Who else in the pediatric ecosystem refers

Pediatricians are the anchor, but the list is longer: developmental pediatricians, pediatric ENTs and audiologists (speech), pediatric neurologists (ABA and behavioral), school psychologists and counselors, early intervention coordinators, daycare and preschool directors, and other therapy practices with waitlists (a speech practice that's full will refer to another). Build the list across all of them.

Measuring the channel

Referring pediatric offices, patients per office per month, time from referral to first visit, and closed-loop rate. The offices that send two or more per month are the relationship priority. The offices that engaged but haven't referred are the follow-up priority.
