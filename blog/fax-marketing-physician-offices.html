Fax Isn't Dead: Why It Still Works for Reaching Physician Offices in 2026

September 7, 2026·3 min read

Every year someone declares fax dead. Every year the fax machine in the pediatrician's office keeps printing. Here's why that matters for referral marketing.

Why physician offices still read faxes

Referral workflows in most outpatient practices are built around fax. Referrals come in by fax. Records go out by fax. Prior auths, lab orders, and insurance correspondence still move by fax in a large share of offices. That means someone in the office is assigned to read the fax queue, every day, and that person is very often the referral coordinator or office manager: exactly who you want to reach.

Compare that to email, which most physician offices don't publish and don't check for marketing, or phone, where you're competing with patients for the front desk's attention. Fax lands on the desk of the person who routes referrals, in the format they already process referrals in.

What the law requires

The Junk Fax Prevention Act and the TCPA govern unsolicited advertisement faxes. In practice, compliant fax outreach means:

  • An established business relationship or permission, or content that isn't an "advertisement" under the rules. Informational communications about clinical services and referral availability are treated differently from promotional advertisements, but the line is fact-specific. Get counsel's view on your specific content.
  • A clear opt-out notice on the first page: a cost-free way to opt out, available 24 hours a day, with the number to use.
  • Honoring opt-outs within 30 days, and maintaining a suppression list across every campaign.
  • Accurate sender identification in the header: your name, fax number, and date and time.

Fax outreach done carelessly generates complaints and legal exposure. Done correctly, it's a routine business communication that offices expect.

What a fax that gets acted on looks like

Most marketing faxes fail because they look like marketing. They have a logo, a tagline, a paragraph about the practice's mission, and no clear next step. The referral coordinator glances at it and moves on.

A fax that works looks like a referral form, because that's the document the coordinator already knows how to use. It has:

  • A headline that says what you take. "Now accepting new pediatric speech therapy referrals, ages 1 to 18, Virginia Medicaid and Anthem."
  • Who to refer. Two or three sentences describing the patient profile.
  • How to refer. A fax-back form with patient name, DOB, insurance, reason for referral, and referring provider. Fillable and faxable, right there.
  • What happens next. "Patients seen within 5 business days. Consult note faxed back within 48 hours of first visit."
  • One contact. Name, direct phone, fax.
  • The opt-out line.

That's it. One page. It's a tool, not an ad.

Sequencing

Fax alone gets a response rate. Fax followed by a voicemail to the office manager three days later, followed by a second fax two weeks later, gets a much better one. In our campaigns, the multi-touch sequence typically produces two to three times the engagement of any single channel. The fax is the artifact that stays on the desk; the voicemail is what makes them look for it.

Measuring it

Track by office: delivered, opened (where the platform reports it), faxed back, called in, referred. Segment by specialty and geography. In most markets, two or three specialties will dramatically outperform the others, and that's where the second wave goes.
